**Spatial principles**

**Familiarity must coexist with wrongness.** Why: pure strangeness is noise, but familiar patterns violated in small ways are uncanny. Example: Level 0’s office-retail grammar plus impossible scale. In game: use recognisable typologies, then bend proportions, topology, or use patterns.  

**Use one dominant spatial thesis per region.** Why: memorable levels compress to a single image and feeling. Example: yellow offices; flooded tile halls. In game: every major zone should fit in one sentence and one silhouette.  

**Repetition must include drift.** Why: repetition induces monotony; drift induces suspicion. Example: recurring room shells with altered ceilings or wall spacing. In game: keep modules related, not identical.  

**Let architecture imply former function.** Why: purposeless randomness is forgettable; implied function invites reconstruction. Example: a service corridor, showroom, locker wing, motel run. In game: design spaces as if they once worked.  

**Hide the horizon of the floorplan.** Why: complete readability collapses dread. Example: walls that reveal only the next uncertainty. In game: long oblique sightlines, partial vistas, broken visibility.  

**Never let the player fully verify the overall size.** Why: infinity is most frightening when inferred from failed measurement rather than announced. Example: route loops that do not reconcile. In game: contradictory odometer, map mismatch, delayed acoustic echoes.  

**Non-Euclidean effects should create doubt, not puzzles first.** Why: puzzle logic domesticates impossibility. Example: a corridor returning to itself one day later. In game: impossible returns before any mechanic language explains them.  

**Dead ends should feel plausible until they become philosophically insulting.** Why: absurdity is stronger when it arrives late. Example: ten ordinary dead ends, then one that contains another corridor behind the same wall. In game: escalate wrongness gradually.  

**Scale changes should be rare and devastating.** Why: constant spectacle desensitises. Example: an ordinary office opening into a hangar-sized duplicate of itself. In game: reserve macro-scale shocks for narrative thresholds.  

**Preserve navigation scars.** Why: players bond to remembered routes. Example: a stain, humming ballast, warped corner. In game: give spaces unique micro-anchors players can learn.  

**Sensory principles**

**Make sound a structural material.** Why: hum, reverb, vent noise, drips, distant machinery all imply scale and occupancy. Example: fluorescent buzz louder than expected. In game: treat ambience as navigation and dread, not as wallpaper.  

**Silence should be an event.** Why: removing a constant hum produces alarm stronger than adding a stinger. Example: one corridor where lights buzz but make no sound. In game: dynamic subtraction beats cheap scare music.  

**Smell should be implied through environment and prose.** Why: olfactory imagination increases embodiment. Example: damp carpet, chlorine, dust, hot ballast. In game: text fragments, character breath, visual decay, condensation.  

**Texture must feel slightly over-specific.** Why: specificity makes emptiness believable. Example: wet carpet depth, powdery ceiling dust, sticky handrails. In game: material response in footsteps, reflections, and movement drag.  

**Lighting should be functional before expressive.** Why: ordinary fluorescents are scarier than theatrical horror rigs. Example: cheap office panels, emergency strips, sodium spill. In game: use believable lighting infrastructure first.  

**Colour palettes should be narrow and sickly.** Why: strong chromatic identities make levels mnemonic and emotionally sealing. Example: mono-yellow; pool-blue; nicotine beige. In game: limit palette families, then let rare accent colours matter.  

**Audio distance should lie occasionally.** Why: uncertain depth makes scale feel wrong. Example: footsteps too near for their reverb. In game: slightly inconsistent propagation in key spaces.  

**Mechanical life beats biological life.** Why: humming systems imply maintenance, schedule, and hidden process. Example: ventilation continuing in an “abandoned” block. In game: fans, pumps, relays, lifts, vending units.  

**Do not overscore emotion.** Why: the player must hear the building think. Example: ambient-only sequence. In game: sparse music reserved for ontological shifts, not basic tension.  

**Let the player hear what they cannot verify.** Why: unconfirmed stimuli preserve ambiguity. Example: a trolley wheel, distant phone ring, water slap. In game: sounds that never resolve into enemy encounters.  

**Temporal principles**

**Time must become blurry.** Why: liminal unease intensifies when duration is uncertain. Example: clocks disagree; hunger cues desynchronise. In game: unreliable diegetic time markers.  

**Stasis should be periodically contradicted by tiny maintenance.** Why: the “still cared for” trace is more disturbing than ruin alone. Example: one fresh bulb in a dead wing. In game: selective upkeep without visible workers.  

**Decay should be uneven.** Why: uniform ruin looks decorative; uneven ruin implies unknown traffic. Example: dusty corridor, but one clean handrail. In game: localised usage patterns.  

**History should appear layered, not narrated.** Why: strata create independent timelines. Example: old wallpaper beneath new signage beneath mould. In game: materials from different decades colliding.  

**Routine without actors is prime Backrooms material.** Why: a schedule continuing after its users are gone feels inhuman. Example: PA chime at 3:17 every cycle. In game: repeating service events with no witnesses.  

**False permanence is stronger than obvious collapse.** Why: the Backrooms is threatening because it still works. Example: power on, carpets damp, no people. In game: “live” infrastructure in dead spaces.  

**The environment should remember more than the player does.** Why: amnesic asymmetry creates dependence. Example: rooms return altered, but the building “knows” their prior state. In game: persistent world marks survive player confusion.  

**Use nostalgia as injury, not comfort alone.** Why: bittersweet memory becomes dread when reunion is impossible. Example: school corridor that feels familiar but cannot be placed. In game: strong late-1990s/early-2000s texture with missing social life.  

**Do not resolve every anomaly into cause.** Why: mystery remains powerful when evidence outruns theory. Example: sealed room with recent coffee smell, never explained. In game: leave some phenomena stubbornly uninterpreted.  

**Let later spaces feel like corrupted recollections of earlier ones.** Why: this creates dream logic and self-reference. Example: pool tiles appearing in office ceilings. In game: recursive motif contamination.  

**Narrative principles**

**Absence is stronger than presence.** Why: the player does more imaginative work when nothing is shown. Example: a recently used sink in an empty complex. In game: traces before sighting, if any sighting at all.  

**Questions matter more than answers.** Why: curiosity sustains long-form engagement. Example: why is one corridor warmer? In game: build investigation chains, not lore dumps.  

**Evidence of people is scarier than people themselves.** Why: it proves presence while preserving absence. Example: half-sorted inventory slip. In game: recent-use artefacts, unfinished acts, interrupted routines.  

**Never explain the whole system.** Why: total explanation shrinks scale to mechanic. Example: partial theories from unreliable notes. In game: multiple plausible models, none complete.  

**Use documents as fragments, not expository bins.** Why: documents should intensify mystery and emotional tone. Example: maintenance checklist with missing dates. In game: short, contradictory, material-specific text.  

**A memorable level needs an emotional job.** Why: places are remembered by feeling, not premise alone. Example: Level 0 = threshold isolation; poolrooms = serene dread. In game: define emotion before mechanics.  

**One impossible rule is better than ten quirky ones.** Why: singularity breeds depth; clutter breeds gimmick. Example: nobody can meet in the same place. In game: each region gets one dominant ontological wound.  

**Treat exposition as archaeology.** Why: the player should excavate, not receive. Example: changed wall paint revealing old numbering. In game: clues embedded at context, macro, and micro level.  

**Restraint makes anomalies expensive.** Why: spectacle is only memorable when rationed. Example: a room of outdoor rain inside a sealed interior after hours of normality. In game: strict anomaly budgeting.  

**Let naming do mnemonic work.** Why: the best names preserve image and tone. Example: “Threshold”, “Sublimity”. In game: label zones with mood-bearing titles, not spreadsheet taxonomy.  

**Systems principles**

**Navigation is the primary drama.** Why: being lost is the antagonist’s method. Example: no stable global orientation. In game: route choice, landmark recall, and inference must be core play.  

**Mapping should be partial, fallible, and personal.** Why: perfect maps kill dread, but no mapping kills mastery. Example: player-drawn sectors that stop matching later. In game: annotation-heavy imperfect cartography.  

**Discovery must feel meaningful, not collectible.** Why: token-chasing trivialises the space. Example: a new kind of corridor is reward enough if it reframes prior understanding. In game: discoveries change theory, route, or emotion.  

**Resources should support atmosphere, not grind.** Why: survival loops can become busywork. Example: batteries, water, chalk, tape, not fifty crafting mats. In game: few high-meaning resources with interpretive consequences.  

**Backtracking should return difference.** Why: repeated routes become uncanny when familiar anchors shift. Example: same stain, different room proportions. In game: mutate revisits selectively.  

**Procedural generation needs macro-grammar.** Why: randomness without typology is mush. Example: office spine, service ring, dead-end nests, void-adjacent halls. In game: hierarchical generation, then local variation.  

**Seed authorship into procedural worlds.** Why: players need rare, unmistakable memories. Example: one impossible atrium per sector. In game: authored signature anomalies injected into generative systems.  

**Do not over-reward speed.** Why: haste reduces perception. Example: sprint-only play misses acoustic and material cues. In game: movement systems that favour attention, not racing.  

**Multiplayer must preserve loneliness.** Why: constant live chatter destroys liminality. Example: delayed signs, asynchronous traces, intermittent contact. In game: ghost-presence more than party-play.  

**Failure should deepen knowledge.** Why: restarting with nothing teaches the wrong lesson. Example: death reveals route inconsistency or new evidence. In game: retain notes, maps, and interpretive gains across deaths.  

**Social and philosophical principles**

**The environment itself is the antagonist.** Why: place, not creature, is the source of pressure. Example: isolation effects, non-Euclidean shifts. In game: most fear events should be architectural or systemic.  

**Civilisation inside the Backrooms must remain small.** Why: large societies normalise the place. Example: tiny refuges, rumours, caches. In game: sparse nodes of human order, never comfortable hubs.  

**Comfort should be temporary and suspicious.** Why: relief increases later unease. Example: a warm break-room with working vending machine. In game: safe-feeling spaces that raise new questions.  

**Wonder and dread should coexist.** Why: awe is part of the hook. Example: impossible submerged halls that are beautiful as well as wrong. In game: vistas that attract and unsettle simultaneously.  

**Scale must routinely eclipse human purpose.** Why: existential discomfort comes from insignificance. Example: infrastructure larger than any plausible user base. In game: reveal sectors that dwarf all known occupation.  

**The player should gradually stop trusting ordinary reality.** Why: the final emotional arc is derealisation, not a single scream. Example: front-world memories start feeling less certain than backrooms routines. In game: long-form erosion of “normal”.  

**Presence should often fail by absence, and absence by presence.** Why: the eerie lives in mismatched expectation. Example: a public atrium with no people; a private bedroom obviously entered. In game: use expected occupancy as a design lever.  

**Let the building feel older than any explanation.** Why: deep time outruns plot. Example: traces from incompatible decades layered together. In game: impossible historical sediment.  

**Do not let mastery become total domestication.** Why: the player may adapt, but should never fully own the space. Example: reliable route to water, but unstable route to meaning. In game: partial competence, not conquest.  

**Endings should feel like withdrawal, compromise, or altered perception, not triumph.** Why: victory is tonally wrong. Example: escape that makes normal spaces feel unreal. In game: resolutions framed as psychological cost, not completion.
