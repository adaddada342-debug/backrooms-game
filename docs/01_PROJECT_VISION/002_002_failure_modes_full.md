## Source Extract
Most Backrooms games fail by translating the premise into the wrong genre. They become chase mazes, co-op streamer bait, or mascot horror in beige corridors. That usually means too much sprinting, too much UI certainty, too many obvious objectives, frequent jumpscares, overexposed entities, and not enough quiet time for the environment to become mentally invasive. Community sentiment consistently shows that many players want the feeling of being alone in a wrong place, not a corridor full of enemies. Research on curiosity and environmental storytelling supports this: fear grows when the player is allowed to inspect, infer, compare, and doubt. citeturn23search3turn23search4turn23search20turn43view0turn41view2

Backrooms lore fails when it confuses accumulation with depth. Common errors include entity inflation, too many named groups, over-systematised survival guides, arbitrary level gimmicks, pseudo-SCP cataloguing without thematic purpose, and excessive explanation of rules that should remain partly inferred. Wikidot’s own writing guidance explicitly rejects “simply jamming more details in” and warns that too many quirky elements make a page unfocused. Fandom quality discussions similarly complain that many weak levels are just generic buildings with “abandoned” in the name and a few entities added on top. citeturn14view1turn14view3turn13view0

Procedural generation fails when it uses randomness instead of *grammar*. Purely random room assembly quickly destroys the affect because real unease depends on expectation formation and violation. If every room is equally strange, nothing is strange. If every corridor is functionally identical, nothing can be remembered. Research on uncanny places points toward deviation-from-familiarity, not noise, while game-exploration research points toward authored curiosity triggers such as obstructed views, extreme points, spatial connections, and purposeful out-of-place elements. Good procedural Backrooms design therefore needs stable typologies, recurrence, long-range motifs, and carefully rationed anomalies. citeturn27view1turn42view0turn44search0turn44search8

Horror games in general fail when they flatten all emotion into “danger now”. The Backrooms specifically needs a broader palette: curiosity, unease, comfort, routine, doubt, suspicion, obsession, scale shock, and only intermittent acute fear. If the player is always in crisis, they never enter the more important states: pattern-seeking, ritualisation, derealisation, or existential fatigue. The environment should first become familiar enough that its wrongness can start to work on the player from the inside. citeturn35view1turn29view1turn32search17turn41view3

A concise fault map is useful:

| Domain | Common mistake | Why it weakens the Backrooms | Better alternative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Games | Constant chasing | Converts existential dread into ordinary threat | Long quiet exploration punctuated by ambiguous incidents |
| Games | Fast movement and stamina loops | Prevents environmental absorption | Slower, texture-aware locomotion with moments of stillness |
| Games | Loud UI, quest markers, objective spam | Restores certainty too quickly | Partial maps, inferred goals, diegetic notes |
| Lore | Everywhere has monsters | Fills the absence that creates dread | Let many spaces remain empty or only *possibly* occupied |
| Lore | Excessive canon explanation | Shrinks mystery | Keep rules local, partial, and revisable |
| Lore | Gimmick levels with no emotional thesis | Memorable image never forms | One strong contradiction, one strong sensory profile |
| Procedural generation | Uniform randomness | No legibility, no memory, no meaningful deviation | Rule-based repetition with curated anomalies |
| Procedural generation | Small-room tiling only | Scale feels fake and gamey | Alternate micro-rooms, macro-halls, voids, service zones |
| Horror design | Jump-scare dependence | Short-term startle replaces slow dread | Audio ambiguity, absence, traces, delayed payoffs |
| Horror design | Combat or boss logic | Reframes the game around mastery and victory | Navigation, interpretation, endurance, cartography |

## Design Rules
- Preserve the Backrooms as an affective, architectural, liminal experience rather than a monster-first horror game.
- Every feature must serve scale, absence, isolation, mystery, uncertainty, exploration, or spatial wrongness.
- Do not solve unease with constant threat, lore dumping, entities, or objective spam.

## Implementation Notes
- Prefer data-driven definitions, stable schemas, and testable systems.
- Any future code must be traceable back to a design rule or emotional target.
- If a mechanic makes the player feel powerful, safe, or fully certain, justify it as temporary and suspicious.

## Codex Instructions
- Read related files before implementation.
- Do not invent new mechanics that contradict the emotional target.
- When uncertain, preserve ambiguity and ask for a design decision rather than filling with generic horror.
