## Source Extract
There is still no single formal discipline called “Backrooms studies”, so several conclusions here are necessarily synthetic rather than direct laboratory findings about the phenomenon itself. Some useful academic sources were accessible only through abstracts or snippets, and community evidence is heterogeneous because the Backrooms has multiple canons and anti-canon communities with conflicting preferences. The strongest conclusions, however, are unusually convergent across sources: familiar-but-wrong architecture, ambiguous threat, forced isolation, uncanny deviation, nostalgic incompletion, traces of recent human presence, and curiosity sustained by partial explanation are the central engines of Backrooms affect. citeturn22view0turn24search3turn27view1turn35view1turn39view0turn43view0

## Design Rules
- Preserve the Backrooms as an affective, architectural, liminal experience rather than a monster-first horror game.
- Every feature must serve scale, absence, isolation, mystery, uncertainty, exploration, or spatial wrongness.
- Do not solve unease with constant threat, lore dumping, entities, or objective spam.

## Implementation Notes
- Prefer data-driven definitions, stable schemas, and testable systems.
- Any future code must be traceable back to a design rule or emotional target.
- If a mechanic makes the player feel powerful, safe, or fully certain, justify it as temporary and suspicious.

## Codex Instructions
- Read related files before implementation.
- Do not invent new mechanics that contradict the emotional target.
- When uncertain, preserve ambiguity and ask for a design decision rather than filling with generic horror.
